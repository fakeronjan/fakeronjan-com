# {{ title }}

<p>A few weeks ago, we <a href="/blog/ua-nba-conference-finals-game-by-game-series-stats/" title="">took a look</a> at the NBA Conference Finals to see whether there were any interesting trends we could pick up game-by-game that weren't well-known.  However, that was really an offshoot of a bigger question: does the NBA Finals 2-3-2 format have an impact on the actual results of the series?</p>
<p>There are two theories here.</p>
<p>Theory One: In giving the lower seeded team three straight home games in the middle, it's giving them a huge <strong>disadvantage</strong>.  Basically, it puts the pressure on them to win two games on the road, because it is very difficult to beat a quality team three times in a row, even at home.<br/><em>Support: Until 2004, no team had ever won at home in games 3, 4, and 5.</em></p>
<p>Theory Two: In giving the lower seeded team three straight home games in the middle, it's giving them a huge <strong>advantage</strong>.  Basically, if they are able to steal one of the first two games, they can then take the series without having to leave home.<br/><em>Support: This has been done three times in the past 9 seasons:<span style="font-size: 1em; line-height: 1.5"> 2004 Pistons over the Lakers, the 2006 Heat over the Mavs, and the 2012 Heat over the Thunder.</span></em><br/><span style="font-size: 1em; line-height: 1.5"><br/></span>Let's dive into some quick numbers!  All figures are from the 1985-2012 seasons, as 1985 was the year that the NBA Finals switched from a 2-2-1-1-1 format to the 2-3-2 format.</p>
<p><u>Average Series Length</u><br/>Conference Finals: 5.7<br/>NBA Finals: 5.6</p>
<p>This does not appear to significantly affect the length of the series.  However, there is a more interesting story here:</p>
<p><strong>16%</strong> of Conference Finals end in sweeps, and <strong>23%</strong> go to game 7<br/><strong>14%</strong> of NBA Finals end in sweeps, and <strong>14%</strong> go to game 7</p>
<p>So in the end, it looks like the 2-3-2 format in the NBA Finals trades out 4-game-sweeps and 7-game-series in exchange for middle-ground series.  It's tough to generate a causal link, of course, but w<span style="font-size: 1em; line-height: 1.5">e can build on this by bringing back three builds from the previous post:</span></p>
<figure><img alt="" loading="lazy" src="/blog/img/ua/nba-finals-does-2-3-2-format-have-an-impact/3246364_orig.png"/></figure>
<p>This diagram lays out the percentage of all series that have the given score.  So the NBA Finals is more likely to be 1-0, 1-1, 2-1, and 3-1 at each respective stage than the Conference Finals.  The 3-1 split, in particular, is fascinating.  Perhaps it suggests that if the lower seed is down, 2-1, they don't have as much of a sense of urgency in game 4 because they know that game 5 is also on their home court?</p>
<figure><img alt="" loading="lazy" src="/blog/img/ua/nba-finals-does-2-3-2-format-have-an-impact/2768761_orig.png"/></figure>
<p>This time, we're looking at the odds that the higher seed wins the entire series given a particular score.  When the road team wins game 1, like the Spurs did last night, it's much <em>less </em>of a boon to their chances than it is in the 2-2-1-1-1 format.  Clearly again speaking to the rarity of sweeping games 3, 4, and 5.</p>
<figure><img alt="" loading="lazy" src="/blog/img/ua/nba-finals-does-2-3-2-format-have-an-impact/9252875_orig.png"/></figure>
<p>This one shows the average length of the series given a score, and I don't think anything interesting is going on here.</p>
<p>So, do we have a takeaway?  Maybe.  It does seem that the 2-3-2 format is a disadvantage to the lower seed, not an advantage.  And while it may slightly decrease the chances of seeing a series sweep (which the NBA wants to dial down), it also decreases the chances of seeing a series go to 7 games (which the NBA wants to dial up).</p>
<p>The NBA should switch back.</p>
