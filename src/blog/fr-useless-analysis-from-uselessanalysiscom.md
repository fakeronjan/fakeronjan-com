# {{ title }}

<p>Over 10 years ago, I started a website, uselessanalysis.com.</p>
<p>It served as a way for me and a handful of friends to answer questions that we’d always wanted to answer, but that no one else had ever bothered to. Questions that didn’t really <em>matter</em>, at least not in a mass appeal or business value kind of way, but questions that absolutely <em>did</em> matter once you started thinking about them.</p>
<figure><img alt="" loading="lazy" src="/blog/img/fr/useless-analysis-from-uselessanalysiscom/header-2013.06.21.png"/></figure>
<p>Most of the content I contributed to the site was sports-related, but I ventured out into music, video games, TV shows, and even politics over a run that spanned from March 2013 through October 2018.</p>
<p>That site was where I first started running my <a href="https://fakeronjan.com/nfl" rel="noopener" target="_blank">NFL</a> and <a href="https://fakeronjan.com/nba" rel="noopener" target="_blank">NBA</a> power rankings. It’s where I first started picking my <a href="https://fakeronjan.com/video-games" rel="noopener" target="_blank">video game of the year</a>. And it’s a source of a handful of posts that I republished on <a href="/blog/">this site</a>.</p>
<p>It was fun. I learned a ton. And oh, my, did I do some truly useless analysis. And while I’ve already ported most of my favorite content from that site over to this one over the past few years, I thought it’d be fun today to call back to my favorite pieces that were lost when I shut that site down.</p>
<p>Without further ado, I present you the <strong>five most useless analyses from uselessanalysis.com</strong>.</p>
<p><strong>5. Correctly predicting Russia’s gold medal count in the 2014 Winter Olympics (February 2014)</strong></p>
<p>Even though I don’t really have any interest in the Olympics, and even though I have literally no experience in analyzing data from the Olympics, I decided to get into the prognostication game for the 2014 Sochi games.</p>
<p>I used the following highly scientific process:</p>
<ul><li><p class="" style="white-space:pre-wrap;">I found the % of total gold medals that Russia won in prior Winter Olympics in the past five events: 18% (1994), 13% (1998), 6% (2002), 10% (2006), and 3% (2010)</p></li><li><p class="" style="white-space:pre-wrap;">I conservatively estimated a 2014 forecast to be around 5% of total golds, assuming that 2010 was a uniquely “down year” but accounting for a general downward trend</p></li><li><p class="" style="white-space:pre-wrap;">I then noted that in 2002 and 2010, Russia had to travel halfway around the world to Salt Lake City and Vancouver, respectively, so I pulled the forecast up to 10% of total golds to adjust out a travel penalty for Russia’s poor performance in 2002 and 2010</p></li><li><p class="" style="white-space:pre-wrap;">Next, I looked at prior hosts to understand the typical lift for a host country compared to the prior and following Olympics, and I found that it varied between a 1% and 8% lift in % of golds won</p></li><li><p class="" style="white-space:pre-wrap;"> I took up Russia’s forecast by an additional conservative 3%, finalizing my forecast at 13% of all gold medals taken by Russia</p></li><li><p class="" style="white-space:pre-wrap;">There were 98 events scheduled in 2014, so 13% of 98 events = 13 gold medals</p></li></ul>
<p>I nailed it. Wow. I can’t believe that worked.</p>

<p><strong>4. Analyzing how much Apu from <em>The Simpsons </em>overcharges at the Kwik-E-Mart (April 2014)</strong></p>
<p>This all came from the episode <em>Homer and Apu</em> in which Apu has this exchange with his customers:</p>
<p><strong>Man 1: </strong>I need one 29-cent stamp.<br/><strong>Apu: </strong>That's $1.85.<br/><strong>Man 2: </strong>I'll have $2.00 worth of gas, please.<br/><strong>Apu: </strong>$4.20.<br/><strong>Martin: </strong>How much is your penny candy?<br/><strong>Apu: </strong>Surprisingly expensive!</p>
<p>So how much is it, really? They never told us!</p>
<p>Well, this one’s easy and we can just apply a linear regression analysis to project how much Martin would need to pay for penny candy.</p>
<figure><img alt="" loading="lazy" src="/blog/img/fr/useless-analysis-from-uselessanalysiscom/5550892-511.png"/></figure>
<p>Including rounding, the answer is $1.47. Pretty pricey.</p>
<p>So Apu’s charging much, much <a href="https://www.nerdwallet.com/article/small-business/credit-card-processing-fees" rel="noopener" target="_blank">more than any credit card company</a> does per transaction. And that’s not even the <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Problem_with_Apu" rel="noopener" target="_blank">biggest problem with Apu</a>, amirite?</p>
<p><strong>3. Analyzing whether there really is a “Madden curse” in the NFL (August 2013)</strong></p>
<p>A decade or so, there was a lot of intense talk about the “Madden curse”.</p>
<p>The concept is similar to the <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sports_Illustrated_cover_jinx" rel="noopener" target="_blank"><em>Sports Illustrated</em> cover jinx</a>: a player who dons the cover of the annual <em>Madden NFL</em> video game is likely to have a down season, and thus, it’s bad for him and his teammates to be featured.</p>
<p>Since a player will only be featured on the cover if they have a great season, many fans chalked this up to simple “regression to the mean”. It’s tough to top a great season, even if you’re a great player.</p>
<p>I wanted to dig deeper, so I did. I looked at all 14 of the <em>Madden NFL</em> games that had released to date and I looked at how each cover athlete’s <em>team</em> performed in the year that their star was on the cover.</p>
<figure><img alt="" loading="lazy" src="/blog/img/fr/useless-analysis-from-uselessanalysiscom/8822717_orig.png"/></figure>
<p>Only two of these teams won a playoff game - the 2006 Seahawks and the 2009 Cardinals - and only the 2007 Titans improved their performance in the following year. So at the time, you could definitely argue that there was a Madden curse - and it not only impacted the player, but his whole team as well!</p>
<p>Patrick Mahomes ultimately <a href="https://www.espn.com/college-sports/story/_/id/28624042/patrick-mahomes-breaks-madden-curse-fulfills-high-school-disney-world-dream" rel="noopener" target="_blank">broke the “curse”</a> after winning the Super Bowl in February 2020, and no one talks about the Madden curse anymore. Oh well, fun times.</p>
<p><strong>2. A team of Michael Jordans takes on the Dream Team (December 2013)</strong></p>
<p>I had just gotten a brand new PS4 and <em>NBA 2K14</em>, and I needed to see what this new game and system was capable. I thought I’d test a few wacky scenarios in the game, and one of them was:</p>
<p><em>What would happen if Isiah Thomas were put on the Dream Team?</em></p>
<p>Well, we know what would happen. Michael Jordan wouldn’t have joined. But I took it a step further:</p>
<p><em>I don't think that's where the story ends. You see, Michael Jordan would be so angry that he'd make it his mission to prove that the Dream Team made a mistake in choosing Isiah over him.</em></p>
<p><em>Since he has a competitive disorder and a penchant for making the impossible possible, I submit that Jordan would find a way to clone himself in order to field a full team of Jordans to enter the 1992 Olympics (for his home country, </em><a href="http://www.jordan.com/" rel="noopener" target="_blank" title=""><em>Nike</em></a><em>) and beat the Dream Team.</em></p>
<p>It’s a stupid premise, but it was actually pretty easy to set up. That version of <em>NBA 2K</em> (and every version that’s released since) has historical teams that you can modify, so I was able to reasonably recreate both the Dream Team - sans Charles Barkley - and a team of nearly all-Jordans.</p>
<p>I then watched these teams play a full game, and Team Jordan won it. It wasn’t quite <a href="https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=m3q3ZoJyirw" rel="noopener" target="_blank"><em>Fumble Dimension</em></a> level craziness, but it sure was useless.</p>

<p><strong>1. I watch <em>Street Fighter II</em> play itself to determine the best CPU-controlled fighter (January 2016)</strong></p>
<p>Yeah, this one is going to be hard to top.</p>
<p>As a kid, my dad would hide my Super Nintendo controllers if I played too much. Which I did, always.</p>
<p>For a while, we’d play the game of cat and mouse. I’d find the controllers and sneak in a gaming session. He’d find out and would hide them somewhere else. Eventually, the hiding spots got too good, and I was at a loss. I couldn’t, like, <em>not</em> play Super Nintendo, right?</p>
<p>So I’d watch the games in demo mode. And a lot of the time, the demo that I’d watch was <em>Street Fighter II</em>. Look, it was better than nothing, okay?!</p>
<p>After watching enough of this back in the day, I became fixated on a simple question, “who is the computer best with?”</p>
<p>Decades later, I was going to find out. I decided to watch the computer play itself 60 times, and then I’d run analysis to see which character performed the best.</p>
<p>It wasn’t a perfect setup. I couldn’t tell the game which matchups to show, so it was up to chance. Some fighters would be featured more than others, and I couldn’t see every possible matchup play out. Sometimes the game would put a character against themselves, giving me no information at all. Beyond that, it was also hard to tell at times who was winning before the demo timer ran out, so I had to allow for ties.</p>
<p>Still, it was enough. I tracked the results in a spreadsheet. I ran an optimization algorithm that accounted for strength of schedule. And I concluded that Guile was the best <em>Street Fighter II</em> character, at least when controlled by the computer.</p>

<p>I don’t know that I’ll ever do anything this useless again. Definitely the highlight of uselessanalysis.com.</p>
